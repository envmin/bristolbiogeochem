About us

The Environmental Mineralogy Group is part of the School of Earth Sciences at the University of Bristol. We study natural processes involving iron minerals. Our main focus is how microbes and minerals interact, and how living things make minerals, a process called biomineralisation.

We combine laboratory experiments with advanced spectroscopy and microscopy to reveal how bacteria produce minerals. We also explore how these biogenic (microbe-made) minerals can be used for metal recovery, cleaning up water and low-cost energy applications.

Research

Biogeobatteries

Some minerals can take up and give out electrons without their structure changing, a bit like a rechargeable battery. We call them biogeobatteries. They are mixed-valence minerals, which means they contain iron or other elements in more than one charge state. Examples include iron oxides, clays, sulphides and green rust. These minerals are widespread underground and may drive significant transfers of energy. Our work aims to understand how microbes access these minerals. This could unlock their potential for low-cost, low-power energy storage in remote places.

Metal recovery and remediation

Iron minerals such as magnetite and ferrihydrite can bind metals to their surfaces and lock them in place. We test iron minerals made by microbes (biogenic) and those formed without them (abiogenic). We measure how well they capture critical metals, which are metals vital to modern technology, such as cobalt, zinc and vanadium. We also test how well they capture toxic contaminants such as arsenic, uranium, chromium and cadmium. The aim is to develop new ways to recover resources from waste and to improve how polluted water is cleaned up.

57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy and data analysis

Mössbauer spectroscopy uses gamma rays to probe iron atoms in terms of oxidation state and mineral composition. We are developing new methods for analysing the data from this technique through a web-based platform. It compares users' own data with a growing database, so that both simple and complex samples can be analysed and interpreted quickly.

Environmental magnetism and climate

Some bacteria gain energy from iron, and in doing so they produce magnetic minerals. We want to explain how this happens in the environment and how it is linked to climate change. High rainfall creates anoxic conditions, meaning there is little or no oxygen. These conditions suit iron-reducing bacteria, which form magnetite. When rainfall is low, oxygen is present, the bacteria slow down or stop, and little magnetite forms. As a result, changes in the magnetic properties of soils and sediments can be used as a record of past rainfall in different environments.

E-waste bioleaching using acidophilic bacteria

Electronic waste, such as old circuit boards, contains high concentrations of valuable metals including gold, copper and rare earth elements. Bioleaching uses microbes to dissolve metals out of waste so they can be recovered. We use acidophilic bacteria, which thrive in very acidic conditions, to leach metals from e-waste. This offers a low-carbon alternative to conventional chemical and thermal recovery. This project is part of BioXtract.

Analytical services and capabilities

We provide 57Fe Mössbauer, UV-Vis and FTIR spectroscopy for industrial and academic partners as well as for our own research. Our instruments in the School of Earth Sciences are set up for iron-bearing samples. They are used to determine iron oxidation state, identify mineral phases and follow reactions as they happen.

  • Industry
  • Academia

57Fe Mössbauer spectroscopy

Fe(II) and Fe(III) proportions, iron mineral phases and their relative abundances, measured from 4.2 to 295 K.

UV-Vis spectrophotometry

Fe(II), total iron, optical density of microbial cultures and other analytes, in 96-well plates or cuvettes.

FTIR spectroscopy

In situ ATR measurements of reactions between solid precipitates and different reagents.

University of Bristol 150th anniversary

At the 150th anniversary of the University of Bristol, James Byrne, Sara Attfield, Hannah Koepnick, Guanlin Li, Naijin Wu and Emile Brunet from the Environmental Mineralogy Group presented some of their work on rusty iron oxides and how rust might not be as bad as you think! We showed how some iron minerals are magnetic, and also how they can be used to clean up water polluted with heavy metals.

Festival of Nature 2025

The Festival of Nature is the UK's largest free celebration of the natural world. It is run by the Natural History Consortium every June across Bristol and Bath. In June 2025 we ran a stand where visitors could explore magnetic iron minerals and how they can help clean up polluted water.

James also gave a talk on the importance of iron as part of the festival's "In a Nutshell" presentations.
